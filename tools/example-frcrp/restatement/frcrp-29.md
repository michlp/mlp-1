# Motion for a judgment of acquittal — Fed. R. Crim. P. 29

Register R2. Source: Fed. R. Crim. P. 29, as published 2026-08-26.

## Before submission to the jury

Under Rule 29(a), the court must enter a judgment of acquittal of any offense
for which the evidence is insufficient to sustain a conviction. The court must
do so on the defendant's motion. The motion may be made after the government
closes its evidence or after the close of all the evidence.

The court may on its own consider whether the evidence is insufficient to
sustain a conviction.

If the court denies a motion for a judgment of acquittal at the close of the
government's evidence, the defendant may offer evidence. The defendant need not
have reserved the right to do so.

## Reserving decision

Under Rule 29(b), the court may do all of the following:

- Reserve decision on the motion
- Proceed with the trial, where the motion is made before the close of all the
  evidence
- Submit the case to the jury
- Decide the motion, either before the jury returns a verdict or after it
  returns a verdict of guilty or is discharged without having returned a
  verdict.

If the court reserves decision, it must decide the motion on the basis of the
evidence at the time the ruling was reserved.

## After a jury verdict or discharge

Under Rule 29(c)(1), a defendant may move for a judgment of acquittal, or
renew that motion. A defendant may do so within 14 days after a guilty verdict
or after the court discharges the jury, whichever is later.

Under Rule 29(c)(2), if the jury has returned a guilty verdict, the court may
set aside the verdict and enter an acquittal. If the jury has failed to return
a verdict, the court may enter a judgment of acquittal.

Under Rule 29(c)(3), a defendant need not move for a judgment of acquittal
before the court submits the case to the jury. That is not a prerequisite for
making the motion after jury discharge.

## Conditional ruling on a motion for a new trial

Under Rule 29(d)(1), if the court enters a judgment of acquittal after a guilty
verdict, the court must also conditionally determine whether any motion for a
new trial "should" be granted. The question is whether a new trial is to be granted if the judgment of
acquittal is later vacated or reversed. The court must specify the reasons for
the determination.

Under Rule 29(d)(2), the court's order conditionally granting a motion for a
new trial does not affect the finality of the judgment of acquittal.

Rule 29(d)(3)(A) applies where the court conditionally grants a motion for a
new trial and an appellate court later reverses the judgment of acquittal. In
that case the trial court must proceed with the new trial. That applies unless
the appellate court orders otherwise.

Under Rule 29(d)(3)(B), if the court conditionally denies a motion for a new
trial, an appellee may assert that the denial was erroneous. If the appellate
court later reverses the judgment of acquittal, the trial court must proceed as
the appellate court directs.
