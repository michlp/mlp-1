# Preserved terms

A preserved term is a word or phrase that names a legal concept, party, instrument, court, standard, or authority. **You may use a preserved term at every register.** The dictionary does not control it.

The question this file answers is the one that matters at drafting time: *may I replace this word with a simpler one?*

If the word falls into any category below, the answer is no.

---

## The central prohibition

> **Never paraphrase a legal standard, test, or threshold.** No exception, at any register.

A legal standard is not a description you may restate in other words. It is a name for a body of law. *Good cause* means what courts have held it to mean. A writer who swaps it for an ordinary-language paraphrase has not simplified the text — the writer has changed the governing law and hidden the change.

> Not CLW: The court may suspend the rule if it has a good reason.
> CLW: The court may suspend the rule for good cause.

> Not CLW: The judge's decision stands unless it was really unreasonable.
> CLW: The appellate court reviews the decision for abuse of discretion.

> Not CLW: The agency's rule is upheld if it makes sense.
> CLW: The court sets aside the rule only if it is arbitrary and capricious.

At R4 a described equivalent may **follow** the standard. It must never **replace** it.

> R4: The court may suspend the rule for *good cause*. "Good cause" is a legal standard. Courts decide case by case what counts as good cause, so this summary cannot tell you whether a particular reason qualifies.

---

## Use the term the source uses

Where the source selects a term, the restatement uses that term. Do not substitute a synonym, even a term of art that means the same thing in general usage.

> Source: "…the district court shall remand the case…"
> Not CLW: The district court must send the case back.
> CLW: The district court must remand the case.

*Petitioner* and *plaintiff* are not interchangeable. *Vacate* and *set aside* are not interchangeable in every context. Follow the source.

For a term the source **defines**, use the source's exact capitalization, hyphenation, and number. A defined term displaces ordinary meaning within the instrument's scope.

## Context decides

Where a word is a preserved term in one context and ordinary English in another, the context controls.

> CLW: A party must respond to a discovery request within 30 days.
> (*Discovery* is a preserved term here.)

> Not CLW: The discovery of the error occurred after judgment.
> CLW: The party found the error after judgment.
> (Ordinary English. Rewrite it.)

## Glossing by register

| Register | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **R1** | Used plainly. The text is the law. |
| **R2** | Preserved, no gloss. The reader is a lawyer. |
| **R3** | Preserved, glossed at first use. |
| **R4** | Preserved, then described. |

> R2: The court acted *sua sponte*.
> R3: The court acted *sua sponte* — on its own, without a request from either party.
> R4: The court acted on its own. Neither party asked it to. Lawyers call this acting *sua sponte*.

The term survives at every level. Only the surrounding explanation changes.

## Near-misses

Words that look like padding and are not:

| Looks like | Actually |
|---|---|
| *reasonable* | A standard with defined content (category 3) |
| *material* | A standard (category 3) |
| *substantial* | A standard (category 3) |
| *willful*, *knowing* | Mental-state standards (category 3) |
| *clearly erroneous* | A standard of review, not an intensifier (category 2) |
| *service*, *joinder*, *waiver* | Nominalizations that are terms of art |
| *aid and abet*, *pain and suffering* | Doublets that became single terms of art |
| *hand down*, *set aside* | Phrasal verbs that are terms of art |

When in doubt, keep the source's word and gloss it for the register. **Keeping a hard word costs the reader a little. Replacing a term of art deletes a rule.**
