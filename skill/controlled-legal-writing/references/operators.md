# Operators

The operator is the load-bearing word in an operative sentence. It tells the reader whether the text imposes a duty, grants a permission, confers a right, or states a consequence. Get it wrong and you have written a different rule.

CLW approves seven operators and no others.

| Operator | Effect | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **must** | Imposes a duty | The actor is required to act. Failure is a violation. |
| **must not** | Imposes a prohibition | The actor is required not to act. Action is a violation. |
| **may** | Confers discretion | The actor is free to act or not. Neither is a violation. |
| **may not** | Withholds authority | The actor has no power to act. Action is void or a violation. |
| **is entitled to** | Confers a right | The actor may demand the thing, and another party must supply it. |
| **need not** | Negates a duty | The actor is not required to act, but may. |
| **will** | States an automatic consequence | The result follows without anyone exercising judgment. |

Never use: *shall, shall not, should, ought to, is required to, is obligated to, is directed to, it is the duty of, no person shall, is permitted to, has the right to, can, might, could, would.*

---

## Two jobs, two rules

Everything below turns on which job you are doing.

- **Drafting** new operative text — you choose the operator. Rule 3.2 applies. Never write *shall*.
- **Restating** a source someone else wrote — the source chose it. Rule 10.7 applies. You map, and you record the mapping.

Confusing the two is how a restatement acquires a duty the source never imposed.

---

## Negation: the four forms are not interchangeable

English negation attaches to different parts of a modal sentence. Choosing the wrong form inverts the provision.

| Form | Means | Does **not** mean |
|---|---|---|
| **must not** | The actor is forbidden to act | The actor is not required to act |
| **need not** | The actor is not required to act | The actor is forbidden to act |
| **may not** | The actor has no authority to act | The actor might not act |
| **is not entitled to** | The actor may not demand the thing | The actor is forbidden to receive it |

**The most common fidelity failure in the whole standard is writing `must not` for `need not`.** It converts an option into a prohibition.

> Source: "A party is not required to attach the exhibits to the motion."
> Not CLW: A party must not attach the exhibits to the motion.
> CLW: A party need not attach the exhibits to the motion.

**`may not` is prohibitive, never probabilistic.** In ordinary English it can mean "might not." In CLW it never does. To express that something might not happen, do not use an operator at all — write *it is possible that*, or restate.

**No double negatives.**

> Not CLW: No party may fail to serve the notice.
> CLW: Each party must serve the notice.

---

## `will` is not a duty

*Will* states what follows automatically, without anyone choosing.

> CLW: The clerk must enter a default. The court will then set a hearing.
> Not CLW: The party will file a response within 14 days.  ← this is a duty; write *must*

---

## Mapping `shall` in a restatement

*Shall* is the most litigated word in English. Courts have construed it as mandatory, as directory, as simple futurity, and as surplusage — sometimes within one statute. The source's construction governs, not your preference.

| Source | Restatement |
|---|---|
| shall (construed mandatory) | must |
| shall (construed directory) | **Do not map.** State that the source says *shall* and cite the construction. |
| shall not (prohibition) | must not |
| shall not (no authority) | may not |
| may | may |
| is entitled to | is entitled to |
| is not required to | need not |
| should | **Do not map.** State that the source uses a non-mandatory word. |

| shall (declaratory) | the simple present |

Never strengthen: *may* does not become *will*. Never weaken: *shall* in a mandatory clause does not become *may*.

**A declaratory *shall* is not a duty.** *The bill of costs shall be $80.00* states an amount. *A filing made after 5 p.m. shall be considered filed the next day* states what is treated as what. Both take the simple present, and neither carries an operator.

**An inverted conditional *should* is not an operator.** Older instruments write *Should the court desire to hear argument* or *Should a party fail to appear*. The word means *if*. Restate it with *if*, and do not apply the rule above.

Test it by uninverting. If *Should X do Y* reads as *If X does Y*, it is a conditional. If it reads as *X ought to do Y*, it is the non-operative *should*.

> Source: "The court may, in its discretion, award costs."
> Not CLW: The court will award costs.
> CLW: The court may award costs. The decision is within the court's discretion.

## Deciding whether a construction is contested

This is the hard part, and the step most often skipped. Mapping `shall` to `must` is the path of least resistance and it is frequently wrong.

**Start from the right default.** You usually cannot verify how courts in the governing jurisdiction have construed a particular `shall`. When you cannot verify it, **do not map it.** Silence about a contested construction is a fidelity failure; saying you could not verify it is not.

Treat the construction as **contested until shown otherwise** when any of these hold:

| Signal | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| The duty runs to a **court, clerk, or public official** | Courts resist readings that strip jurisdiction or void official acts for a missed step. These are the classic directory cases. |
| The provision sets a **deadline for official action** ("the court shall rule within 30 days") | Almost never enforced by invalidating the late act. |
| The verb is **`shall consider`**, `shall review`, `shall take into account` | Often mandatory as to the act, directory as to the outcome. The two readings differ sharply. |
| **No consequence is stated** for non-compliance | A duty with no sanction is the fact pattern that produces directory readings. |
| The same instrument uses **`shall` and `must`, or `shall` and `may`, inconsistently** | Undercuts any presumption that `shall` is doing mandatory work. |
| The instrument is **old, or drafted before the jurisdiction's style manual** | `Shall` was used for four jobs at once well into the twentieth century. |

Where a signal fires, and you cannot point to authority resolving it, write the provision without choosing:

> The statute states that the agency "shall" publish the notice. Courts have divided on whether this word imposes a duty or states a preference. Refer to [citation] and [citation].

If you have no authority to cite, say that instead. Do not invent one:

> The statute states that the agency "shall" publish the notice. This summary does not resolve whether that word imposes a duty or states a preference. A court's construction may control.

**Record every mapping you do make** in the proposition ledger, with the authority relied on. A mapping is a decision, and an unrecorded decision cannot be checked.

## Drafting: yielding to a governing manual

Rule 3.2 says what to write where the writer is free to choose. It does not displace a drafting office.

Most state legislatures still teach *shall* as the mandatory operator and *may* as the permissive one, and a drafter in one of those offices is required to follow the manual. Where a drafting manual, court rule, or agency directive governs, **that authority controls.** Check the order of precedence before applying Rule 3.2 to text destined for a legislature or agency.

## Every duty needs a bearer

> Not CLW: The notice must be filed within 14 days.
> CLW: The appellant must file the notice within 14 days.

**Do not attach a duty to an inanimate thing.**

> Not CLW: The motion must include a certificate of service.
> CLW: A party who files a motion must include a certificate of service.

The first describes a property of a document. The second imposes a duty on a person, which is what the rule does.

**Where the source does not name a bearer, do not invent one.** Preserve the source's construction and note the gap:

> The rule states that notice must be given. It does not state who must give the notice.

## Quantifiers and connectors

These carry as much legal content as operators, and they are easier to lose because they are small.

| Source | Do not write |
|---|---|
| each party | the parties, all parties |
| any party | a party, every party |
| all of the following | the following |
| one or more of the following | the following |
| at least 14 days | 14 days |
| no more than 30 days | 30 days, about 30 days |

**Connectors.** *And* versus *or* is the difference between a conjunctive and a disjunctive test, which is the difference between two rules. Preserve the source's connector exactly.

**Exclusivity.** *Only, solely, exclusively.* Dropping *only* converts a limitation into a grant.

> Source: "The court may act only on motion."
> Not CLW: The court may act on motion.

**Inclusivity.** *Including* opens a list. *Consisting of* and *means* close one. Do not convert *includes but is not limited to* into *includes* — some jurisdictions treat *includes* as exhaustive.
